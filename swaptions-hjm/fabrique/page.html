Co-terminal Bermudan swaption pricer

The HJM model (mean reversion \lambda, piecewise-constant volatility \sigma(t)) is calibrated live to co-terminal European swaptions quoted in normal volatility; the Bermudan is then priced by a PDE (one factor) or a 2-D backward induction (two factors) and checked by Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo. The two-factor model gives up Jamshidian's trick but fits the whole swaption matrix: it is tested here on a real EUR market. A third model adds a stochastic volatility to capture the smile (Monte Carlo, with the exact two-factor price as control variate). Reviewing the original code revealed a sign error in the zero-coupon bond formula: the page uses the corrected formula (the original one stays available in the one-factor Pricer).

Remark: mean reversion, recalibration and the value of the Bermudan

For a Bermudan swaption, increasing the mean reversion \lambda and recalibrating to the European swaptions generally increases its price. Why?

  • Recalibration keeps the European prices fixed: the rise in \lambda is offset by a rise in \sigma. Each European only sees the total variance of its own exercise date, so its price does not move.
  • Faster mean reversion makes rates less persistent and lowers their correlation across exercise dates: \operatorname{corr}(X_{T_i},X_{T_j})=\sqrt{\alpha(T_i)/\alpha(T_j)}, and for a constant \sigma, \alpha(T_i)/\alpha(T_j)=(e^{2\lambda T_i}-1)/(e^{2\lambda T_j}-1) decreases in \lambda.
  • Choosing among several dates becomes more valuable: if exercising is not worth it today, conditions may be very different at a later date. The Bermudan is worth at least the most expensive European, and the gap (the switch option) grows when the dates decorrelate.

The Bermudan therefore gains more from its exercise flexibility, while the calibration Europeans keep their prices. It is a tendency, not a universal rule: the result depends on the model, the calibration instruments and the structure of the Bermudan. Without recalibration the effect reverses: a higher \lambda with frozen \sigma just damps the volatility, and every price falls.

In one sentence: "After recalibration to the Europeans, higher mean reversion tends to increase the value of the Bermudan, because it reduces the dependence between exercise opportunities and makes the choice of the date more valuable."

1. The HJM framework and the separable volatility

Heath, Jarrow and Morton model the whole curve of instantaneous forward rates f(t,T). Under the risk-neutral measure \mathbb Q, absence of arbitrage (discounted bonds are martingales) fixes the drift once the volatility is chosen:

df(t,T)=\sigma_f(t,T)\left(\int_t^T\sigma_f(t,u)\,du\right)dt+\sigma_f(t,T)\,dW_t .

The model uses the separable volatility \sigma_f(t,T)=\sigma(t)\,e^{-\lambda(T-t)}: a shock at time t moves the forwards of all maturities, exponentially less for distant ones. Writing \sigma_f(t,T)=\eta(t)\,b(T) with \eta(t)=\sigma(t)e^{\lambda t} and b(T)=e^{-\lambda T} makes the model Markovian in a single, driftless Gaussian factor:

X_t=\int_0^t\eta(u)\,dW_u,\qquad X_t\sim\mathcal N\big(0,\alpha(t)\big),\qquad \alpha(t)=\int_0^t\sigma(u)^2e^{2\lambda u}\,du .

This is exactly the Hull–White model dr_t=(\theta(t)-\lambda r_t)\,dt+\sigma(t)\,dW_t with the curve fitted by construction: \lambda is the speed of mean reversion of the short rate, \sigma(t) its instantaneous normal volatility.

2. Zero-coupon bonds and the short rate

Integrating the drift with \bar B(t)=\int_0^tb(u)\,du=(1-e^{-\lambda t})/\lambda and \gamma(t)=\int_0^t\eta(u)^2\bar B(u)\,du gives

f(t,T)=f(0,T)+b(T)\big[\bar B(T)\,\alpha(t)-\gamma(t)+X_t\big],

hence the short rate r_t=f(t,t) and, using \int_t^Tb(u)\bar B(u)\,du=\tfrac12\big(\bar B(T)^2-\bar B(t)^2\big), the bond price P(t,T)=\exp\!\big(-\int_t^Tf(t,u)\,du\big):

\begin{aligned} r_t&=f(0,t)+e^{-\lambda t}\big[\bar B(t)\,\alpha(t)-\gamma(t)\big]+e^{-\lambda t}X_t,\\ P(t,T)&=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\!\Big(-D(t,T)\big[c(t,T)+X_t\big]\Big),\quad D(t,T)=\bar B(T)-\bar B(t),\\ c(t,T)&=\tfrac12\big(\bar B(T)+\bar B(t)\big)\alpha(t)-\gamma(t). \end{aligned}

Bonds are exponential-affine in X_t and decreasing in it: a high factor means high rates. The term c(t,T) is the convexity adjustment that makes P(t,T)/\beta_t, with \beta_t=e^{\int_0^tr_s\,ds}, a martingale. Its sign is exactly what the original code got wrong (section 8).

3. European swaptions: Bachelier and Jamshidian

A payer swaption with expiry T_0, fixed rate K and fixed dates T_1,\dots,T_n (accruals \tau_i) pays \big(1-P(T_0,T_n)-K\sum_i\tau_iP(T_0,T_i)\big)^+. The market quotes it in normal (Bachelier) volatility \sigma_N, with annuity A_0=\sum_i\tau_iP(0,T_i) and forward swap rate S_0=(P(0,T_0)-P(0,T_n))/A_0:

V^{\text{payer}}_0=A_0\Big[(S_0-K)\,\Phi(d)+\sigma_N\sqrt{T_0}\,\varphi(d)\Big],\qquad d=\frac{S_0-K}{\sigma_N\sqrt{T_0}} .

In the model, the payoff is a put with strike 1 on a coupon bond \sum_ic_iP(T_0,T_i), c_i=K\tau_i, c_n=1+K\tau_n. Since every P(T_0,T_i) decreases in the single factor, Jamshidian's trick applies: find x^\star with \sum_ic_iP(T_0,T_i;x^\star)=1, set K_i=P(T_0,T_i;x^\star), and

V^{\text{payer}}_0=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\,\mathrm{ZBP}(T_0,T_i,K_i),\qquad \mathrm{ZBP}=K_iP(0,T_0)\Phi(-h+v)-P(0,T_i)\Phi(-h),

with v=D(T_0,T_i)\sqrt{\alpha(T_0)} and h=\ln\frac{P(0,T_i)}{K_iP(0,T_0)}/v+v/2. The receiver uses calls on the bonds; payer − receiver = forward swap is checked to 10^{-8}.

4. Calibration: the co-terminal bootstrap

Each exercise date T_k carries the European swaption T_k\to T_N. Its model price depends only on \alpha(T_k)=\sum_{j\le k}\sigma_j^2\,\frac{e^{2\lambda T_j}-e^{2\lambda T_{j-1}}}{2\lambda}, i.e. on the segments \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k. Solving the dates in order, each step is a one-dimensional root search (Brent) for \sigma_k, and the fit is exact. The co-terminal Europeans cannot identify \lambda: for any \lambda there is a \sigma(\cdot) that matches them. \lambda must come from other instruments: caps, which are sums of options on short forward rates, identify it, and the Mean reversion tab does so on the real market (card What the market caps say about λ: the caps want \lambda\approx-0.15, the swaptions \lambda\approx-0.04, so a single factor cannot fit both). Its effect on the Bermudan is studied in that tab as well.

5. The Bermudan as an optimal stopping problem

The holder may enter the swap at any T_k, k=1,\dots,m; the exercise value is the underlying co-terminal swap h_k(x)=\big(1-P(T_k,T_N)-K\sum_{T_i>T_k}\tau_iP(T_k,T_i)\big)^+. By dynamic programming,

V_{T_m}=h_m,\qquad V_{T_k}=\max\big(h_k,\;C_k\big),\qquad C_k=\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[e^{-\int_{T_k}^{T_{k+1}}r_s\,ds}\,V_{T_{k+1}}\,\middle|\,X_{T_k}\right],

and V_0\ge\max_kV^{\text{Eur}}_k: the difference is the early-exercise (switch) premium.

6. The PDE

Between exercise dates, by Feynman–Kac, V(t,x) solves

\partial_tV+\tfrac12\eta(t)^2\,\partial_{xx}V-r(t,x)\,V=0,\qquad r(t,x)=A(t)+e^{-\lambda t}x,

solved backwards on x\in[-n\sqrt{\alpha(T_m)},\,n\sqrt{\alpha(T_m)}] with Dirichlet values equal to the intrinsic value, and V\leftarrow\max(V,h_k) at each T_k (the exercise dates lie on the time grid). Each step solves a tridiagonal system (Thomas algorithm, O(n_x)). The \theta-scheme

\frac{V^n-V^{n+1}}{\Delta t}=\theta\,\mathcal L^nV^n+(1-\theta)\,\mathcal L^{n+1}V^{n+1}

is first order for \theta=1 (implicit Euler, the original code) and second order for \theta=\tfrac12 (Crank–Nicolson). Crank–Nicolson is not L-stable: the kink of the payoff at each exercise date excites oscillations, so a few implicit Rannacher steps are taken after every date to damp them while keeping second order.

7. Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo

Paths of X are simulated exactly (Gaussian increments of variance \alpha(T_{k+1})-\alpha(T_k)). Going backwards, the continuation value is estimated by least squares among in-the-money paths, on a polynomial basis \{1,\tilde x,\dots,\tilde x^d\} with \tilde x=x/\sqrt{\alpha(T_k)} (better conditioned); the path exercises when h_k>\hat C_k. Two biases pull in opposite directions:

  • in-sample (high) bias: regressing and pricing on the same paths lets the rule peek at the future;
  • sub-optimality (low) bias: any exercise rule applied to independent paths gives a lower bound of the true price.

The page offers both (option "Regress on independent paths"). Discounting must use the simulated \int r_s\,ds: the page integrates r by the trapezoidal rule on a fine grid, path by path.

8. Code review: the sign error and other findings

Sign error

hjm_1f.py writes P(t,T)=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\!\big(D(t,T)[c(t,T)-X_t]\big): the convexity term c(t,T) has the wrong sign, and short_rate, which is its derivative in T at T=t, inherits it. Section 2 derives the correct sign directly from the HJM drift.

  • Monte Carlo discounting. mc_1f.py freezes the short rate between exercise dates and then discounts everything at r(0) back to today, ignoring the curve: discount factors are off by 0.26 % at 2 years and 0.59 % at 3 years (table "Martingale check"), and the LSMC price is 3 bp of notional too high. Added: X on a fine grid and e^{-\int r} simulated path by path; the LSMC then agrees with the PDE.
  • Regression bias. Added: regression on independent paths (a lower bound) and a choice of polynomial degree.
  • PDE time scheme. Added: Crank–Nicolson with Rannacher steps after each exercise date.
  • Receiver swaption. The Python returns the payer formula; here Jamshidian with calls on the bonds.
  • Freedom. User-set \sigma(t) without calibration, implied normal vols of the model, every grid and numerical choice adjustable.

9. Validation

With the notebook settings, the JavaScript engine reproduces every PDE price of Main.ipynb to the 8th decimal (grids, domains, \lambda from 0 to 0.3, strikes from 2 % to 4 %) and the calibrated \sigma to 10^{-17}. With the corrected formula, Jamshidian, the PDE and the Monte Carlo agree on the European, and the simulated bonds recover the curve within their confidence interval. The whole market, not three quotes

The data are those of the course (EUR, 30 March 2016): the stripped Euribor 6M curve, the 21 × 14 matrix of at-the-money swaption normal volatilities (expiry 1 month to 30 years, tenor 1 to 30 years), the 16 ATM cap volatilities, and the strike smiles. The Bermudan is a payer swaption into a swap ending at T_N, exercisable every year up to the last date you choose, struck at the T_N-year par rate.

  • Volatility segments: one at every expiry of the matrix up to the last exercise date (1M, 2M, 3M, 6M, 9M, 1Y, 18M, 2Y, …), each fitted exactly: on the co-terminal swaption quoted along the smile at the Bermudan's strike (it is not at the money), or on an ATM swaption for the other expiries.

  • Report card: both models are then judged on all 240 swaptions and all 16 caps, including those beyond the window, on which they were not fitted.

Remark: the Europeans do not determine the Bermudan

With one factor, once the co-terminal Europeans are matched, the Bermudan is almost fixed: only \lambda is left, and its effect is studied in the one-factor Mean reversion tab. With two factors, the same Europeans are compatible with very different Bermudans: what matters is how the swap rate at one exercise date co-moves with the swap rate at the next, and that is set by \rho, \lambda_2 and \sigma_2/\sigma_1, which the co-terminals do not see.

  • The premium moves with the dependence structure: with the default inputs (the real market, 11 segments matched exactly) the exercise premium (Bermudan − most expensive European) goes from about 46 bp at \rho=-0.95 (the value fitted on the market) to about 102 bp at \rho=+0.9, and the Bermudan from 0.0483 to 0.0539 (12 %); the single-factor price lies inside this range. The mechanism is the one of the one-factor Mean reversion tab: how much the swap rates of successive exercise dates move together decides how much choosing the date is worth.
  • This is model risk made visible, not a defect: it is exactly the freedom that lets the model fit the rest of the market. The fit of tab Fit to the market is what removes it.

1. From one factor to two

The forward rates are driven by two Brownian motions with correlation \rho, and each factor keeps the separable exponential volatility of the one-factor model:

\sigma_k(t,T)=\sigma_k(t)\,e^{-\lambda_k(T-t)},\qquad k=1,2,\qquad d\langle W^1,W^2\rangle_t=\rho\,dt .

The no-arbitrage drift is \sum_{k,l}\rho_{kl}\,\sigma_k(t,T)\int_t^T\sigma_l(t,u)\,du (with \rho_{kk}=1, \rho_{12}=\rho). Exactly as before, the model is Markovian in two driftless Gaussian factors X^k_t=\int_0^t\sigma_k(u)e^{\lambda_ku}dW^k_u, jointly normal with

\operatorname{Cov}\big(X^k_t,X^l_t\big)=A_{kl}(t)=\rho_{kl}\int_0^t\sigma_k(u)\sigma_l(u)\,e^{(\lambda_k+\lambda_l)u}\,du .

This is the two-factor Hull–White model of the lecture notes (r=r_0+r_1+r_2, dr_k=-\lambda_kr_k\,dt+\sigma_k\,dW^k), where a negative \rho (around -0.9) lets the first factor carry the level of the curve and the second its slope. With \sigma_2=0 it is the one-factor model of the other tab, exactly.

2. Zero-coupon bonds

With \bar B_k(t)=(1-e^{-\lambda_kt})/\lambda_k, D_k(t,T)=\bar B_k(T)-\bar B_k(t) and G_{kl}(t)=\rho_{kl}\int_0^t\sigma_k\sigma_le^{(\lambda_k+\lambda_l)u}\bar B_l(u)\,du,

P(t,T)=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\Big(-\sum_kD_k(t,T)\,X^k_t+\kappa(t,T)\Big), \kappa(t,T)=-\tfrac12\sum_{k,l}\Big[\big(\bar B_k(T)\bar B_l(T)-\bar B_k(t)\bar B_l(t)\big)A_{kl}(t)-D_k(t,T)\,G_{kl}(t)-D_l(t,T)\,G_{lk}(t)\Big].

For one factor \kappa=-D\,c: this is the corrected formula of the other tab (convexity term with the sign of X_t). The check that matters is the martingale defect \mathbb E^{T}\!\big[P(t,b)/P(t,T)\big]-P(0,b)/P(0,T), computed with the closed-form drift of the factors under the T-forward measure: it is zero to machine precision for any parameters.

3. What replaces Jamshidian

Jamshidian's trick rests on one fact: the swap value 1-P(T_0,T_n)-K\sum_i\tau_iP(T_0,T_i) is monotone in the single factor, so a single root x^\star splits it into options on zero-coupon bonds. With two factors the swap value is a function S(x_1,x_2) of the plane and the exercise set \{S>0\} is bounded by a curve: there is no threshold, no common strike K_i, no decomposition (the region plot of the Pricer tab shows the curve). The price is instead the integral

V_0=P(0,T_0)\;\mathbb E^{T_0}\big[S(X_{T_0})^+\big],\qquad X_{T_0}\sim\mathcal N\big(m(T_0;T_0),\,A(T_0)\big),

where the mean m_k(t;T)=-\sum_l\rho_{kl}\int_0^t\sigma_k\sigma_le^{\lambda_ku}\bar B_l(T-u)\,du is the (deterministic) drift of the factors under the T-forward measure. The page evaluates it on a fine uniform grid of the normal law (default 161 × 161 points): the payoff has a kink, and on a kink a fine uniform rule is more accurate than Gauss–Hermite (measured: 4\cdot10^{-4} relative against Jamshidian in the one-factor limit). The bootstrap still works because the price increases with the volatility level, but every Brent step now costs an integral instead of a closed form.

Closed-form approximations exist (conditioning on one factor then applying a Jamshidian-type argument on the other, or Gaussian approximations of the swap rate): they are faster but approximate, and are not used here.

4. Calibration on the whole market

The co-terminal bootstrap is unchanged: for given (\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\rho,\sigma_2/\sigma_1), one root per volatility segment fixes the level of \sigma_1, and that segment is fitted exactly. But the structural parameters are invisible to the co-terminals: any value of them can be compensated by the level. They are identified by the rest of the market, through a global, non-linear fit (Nelder–Mead on the root-mean-square gap in normal volatility; the segments are re-bootstrapped at every evaluation).

The data and what is used. The course data (EUR, 30 March 2016) hold the Euribor 6M curve, 240 ATM swaptions ending within 30 years (expiries 1 month to 25 years, tenors 1 to 30), 16 ATM caps (1 to 30 years) and 13 swaption smiles. The Bermudan (into 10 years, exercisable at 1 to 5 years, struck at the 10-year par rate) has 11 volatility segments, one at every expiry of the matrix up to 5 years (1M, 2M, 3M, 6M, 9M, 1Y, 18M, 2Y, 3Y, 4Y, 5Y), each fitted exactly: on the co-terminal swaption quoted along the smile at the Bermudan's strike (it lies 8 to 53 bp below the forward swap rates, and the quote moves by up to 1.3 bp), or on an ATM swaption. The structural parameters are fitted on 143 ATM swaptions and 6 ATM caps (every expiry up to 5 years), and both models are then judged on all 240 swaptions and 16 caps.

5. The Bermudan on a two-dimensional grid

The one-factor PDE is a tridiagonal system per time step. In two factors the PDE has a cross-derivative and costs a grid of n^2 points per step. But between exercise dates the factors are exactly Gaussian, so the page does not discretise time at all. Working under the T_N-forward measure, on values deflated by P(t,T_N) (martingales: no discount factor to simulate),

U_j(x)=\max\Big(\frac{h_j(x)}{P(T_j,T_N;x)},\;\mathbb E^{T_N}\big[U_{j+1}(X_{T_{j+1}})\,\big|\,X_{T_j}=x\big]\Big),\qquad V_0=P(0,T_N)\,\mathbb E^{T_N}\big[U_1(X_{T_1})\big],

where X_{T_{j+1}}-X_{T_j}\sim\mathcal N\big(m(T_{j+1};T_N)-m(T_j;T_N),\,A(T_{j+1})-A(T_j)\big) independently of the past. The conditional expectation is a Gaussian kernel integrated against bilinear interpolation of U_{j+1} on its grid. The only errors are the grid and the kernel quadrature (measured: 1.2 % at 41 × 61, 0.2 % at 101 × 141). The Longstaff-Schwartz engine runs on the same deflated values with a two-variable polynomial basis, and brackets the quadrature from below (suboptimal exercise rule).

The Europeans do not determine the Bermudan. With the 11 segments of the real case matched exactly, the Bermudan moves from 0.0483 (\rho=-0.95, the fitted value) to 0.0539 (\rho=+0.9), i.e. by 12 %, and its premium from 46 to 102 bp (tab Correlation and what it buys). The single-factor Bermudan sits inside this range. It is the fit to the rest of the market that selects \rho.

7. Validation

  • No arbitrage: the martingale defect is zero (10^{-17}) for generic parameters; the bond formula and the drift of the factors agree.
  • One-factor limit: with \sigma_2=0, the European 2-D integral reproduces Jamshidian (4\cdot10^{-4} relative) and the quadrature reproduces the corrected one-factor PDE (0.2 %).
  • Perfect correlation: \rho=1 with \lambda_1=\lambda_2 and \sigma_1+\sigma_2=\sigma gives the one-factor price.
  • Caps: the closed-form bond put agrees with the 2-D integral of the same payoff to machine precision.
  • Two engines: quadrature and Monte Carlo agree within 0.5 % (the quadrature usually slightly above, the Monte Carlo below: its exercise rule is suboptimal).
  • Two languages: the JavaScript engine of this page and the Python package hjm2f agree to 10^{-13} and better on prices, covariances, caps, smile, segments and bootstrap; the global fit, run in both, returns the same parameters and the same errors.
  • Every number of this page is recomputed by validate_2f.py before publication.

A third model: two factors and a stochastic volatility

The 1- and 2-factor models are Gaussian: their normal volatility is flat in the strike, so they cannot see the smile. This model scales both factors by a stochastic volatility multiplier m_t=\exp(y_t-v(t)), dy=-\kappa y\,dt+\xi\,dZ, correlated with the first factor (\rho_v), and keeps everything else of the 2-factor model (same \lambda_1,\lambda_2,\rho,\sigma_2/\sigma_1). It has no closed form: it is simulated, with the exact 2-factor price as a control variate (price = exact 2F + Monte Carlo difference with and without stochastic volatility, on the same random numbers).

  • What it adds: the smile (three parameters \kappa,\xi,\rho_v fitted on the swaption smiles of the data) and a Bermudan that depends on the volatility dynamics, not only on the Europeans.
  • Same Europeans for all three models: the 11 volatility segments are re-bootstrapped under the stochastic-volatility dynamics so that every co-terminal quote (taken along the smile at the Bermudan's strike) is reproduced; only the dynamics differ.

1. A stochastic volatility on top of two factors

The 1- and 2-factor models have deterministic volatilities, so every swaption has a normal volatility that does not depend on its strike: no smile. The remedy that keeps the HJM structure is a scalar multiplier of both factors,

\sigma_k(t,T)=\sigma_k(t)\,m_t\,e^{-\lambda_k(T-t)},\qquad m_t=e^{\,y_t-v(t)},\quad dy_t=-\kappa y_t\,dt+\xi\,dZ_t,\quad v(t)=\frac{\xi^2\,(1-e^{-2\kappa t})}{2\kappa},

with dZ=\rho_v\,dW^1+\sqrt{1-\rho_v^2}\,dB (B independent of the two Brownian motions of the factors). The compensator v(t) gives \mathbb E[m_t^2]=1: the average variance is that of the 2-factor model, and the stochastic volatility only redistributes it. A log-normal multiplier (as in SABR) was preferred to a square-root one (Heston): on this market, whose smiles are steep at short expiries, the square-root process needed a volatility of volatility so large that the Euler scheme broke down, and the fit was driven to the bounds. Three parameters are added: \kappa, \xi, \rho_v (\rho_v>0: volatility rises with rates, which gives the upward-sloping smiles of the data).

2. Pricing: random covariances, control variate

The HJM drift of the forward rates is a pathwise identity, so the bond formula of the 2-factor model survives, with random covariances: A_{kl}(t)=\rho_{kl}\int_0^t\sigma_k\sigma_l\,e^{(\lambda_k+\lambda_l)u}\,m_u^2\,du, and the same for G_{kl}(t):

P(t,T)=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\Big(-\sum_kD_k(t,T)X^k_t+\kappa(t,T;A_t,G_t)\Big).

The model is simulated under the T^\star-forward measure (values deflated by P(t,T^\star) are martingales, no path-dependent discounting). There X^k has the drift -m_t^2\,\sigma_k e^{\lambda_kt}\sum_l\rho_{kl}\sigma_lB_l(T^\star-t) and y acquires the drift -\xi\rho_v\,m_t\,(\sigma_1B_1(T^\star-t)+\rho\,\sigma_2B_2(T^\star-t)): the change of measure also acts on the volatility, because it is correlated with the first factor. The step is exact in y (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck), and the increments of A, G and of the mean are those of the 2-factor model scaled by m^2, so that with \xi=0 the simulation is the exact Gaussian transition.

Control variate. Run twice on the same random numbers, with the stochastic volatility and with \xi=0, and add the difference to the exact 2-factor price:

\text{price}_{SV}=\text{price}_{2F}^{\text{exact}}+\big(\text{MC}_{SV}-\text{MC}_{\xi=0}\big).

The noise only has to resolve the effect of the stochastic volatility, not the price itself. European swaptions use the exact 2-D integral, caps the closed-form bond put, the Bermudan the 2-D quadrature of the previous tab; Longstaff–Schwartz (basis: bivariate polynomials plus m^2-1) supplies the difference.

3. Calibration

  • Smile (the new parameters). \kappa,\xi,\rho_v are fitted by Nelder–Mead on the 9 smiles of the data that expire within the Bermudan's window (54 points: strikes up to about 3 standard deviations from the forward; further out the prices are below the Monte Carlo resolution), on the shape of the smile (model vol divided by the model's ATM vol, times the market ATM vol). Two different starting points give the same optimum, \kappa=2.29, \xi=0.925, \rho_v=0.41, which is more than can be said of the 2-factor parameters (flat valley, one bound active).
  • Structural parameters. \lambda_1,\lambda_2,\rho,\sigma_2/\sigma_1 are kept at their 2-factor values. A joint re-fit under the stochastic volatility on the 143 swaptions and 6 caps returns the same values (RMSE 4.01 bp against 3.96 at the 2-factor values).
  • Segments. The 11 volatility levels are re-bootstrapped under the new dynamics on the same quotes (co-terminals along the smile at the Bermudan's strike, ATM swaptions for the other expiries), by an over-relaxed fixed-point iteration on the Monte Carlo prices (residual below 0.05 bp). The levels rise by 0 to 13 % relative to the 2-factor model (typically 4 to 6 %), because at equal levels an at-the-money option is cheaper under the multiplier. All three models therefore reproduce the same Europeans: the Bermudan differs by the dynamics only.

6. Validation

  • No arbitrage: \mathbb E^{T^\star}[P(t,b)/P(t,T^\star)]=P(0,b)/P(0,T^\star) holds to within the Monte Carlo error (2\cdot10^{-4} relative) for \xi=0 and for stochastic-volatility parameters with \rho_v\ne0, which tests the drift of the factors and of the volatility under the forward measure. (A first version of the bond formula, with a slip in the cross terms of G, failed this test by 10^{-3}, which is 20 % on the price of a 5-year swaption: the test is sensitive.)
  • Gaussian limit: with \xi=0 the simulation reproduces the exact 2-D integral of the 2-factor model within the Monte Carlo error, and the Bermudan quadrature is unchanged.
  • Two languages: the JavaScript engine of this page and the Python package hjm3sv agree within the Monte Carlo error (0.26 bp on the implied vols, 0.06 % on the Bermudan) with different random-number generators.
  • Every number of this tab is recomputed by run_sv.py and checked by validate_2f.py.

7. Limits

  • One multiplier drives both factors: the smile is explained, but the term structure of the smile (long expiries) relies on a single mean reversion; strikes beyond about 3 standard deviations are not fitted.
  • With \rho_v<0 the log-normal volatility has a forward-measure drift that can explode; the simulation clips y at ±4 and the fitted \rho_v is positive, so the clip is inactive on the real case, but the page accepts \rho_v<0.
  • The ATM structure of the 2-factor model is not re-fitted jointly with the smile. The caps' own smiles (strikes −0.5 % to 5 %) are not used and would be the natural next test.
  • The Bermudan is a Monte Carlo price: it carries noise, a lower-bias regression policy and no dual upper bound.
