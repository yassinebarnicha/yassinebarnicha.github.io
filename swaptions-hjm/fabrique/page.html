Remark: mean reversion, recalibration and the value of the Bermudan

For a Bermudan swaption, increasing the mean reversion \lambda and recalibrating to the European swaptions generally increases its price. Why?

  • Recalibration keeps the European prices fixed: the rise in \lambda is offset by a rise in \sigma. Each European only sees the total variance of its own exercise date, so its price does not move.
  • Faster mean reversion makes rates less persistent and lowers their correlation across exercise dates: \operatorname{corr}(X_{T_i},X_{T_j})=\sqrt{\alpha(T_i)/\alpha(T_j)}, and for a constant \sigma, \alpha(T_i)/\alpha(T_j)=(e^{2\lambda T_i}-1)/(e^{2\lambda T_j}-1) decreases in \lambda.
  • Choosing among several dates becomes more valuable: if exercising is not worth it today, conditions may be very different at a later date. The Bermudan is worth at least the most expensive European, and the gap (the switch option) grows when the dates decorrelate.

The Bermudan therefore gains more from its exercise flexibility, while the calibration Europeans keep their prices. It is a tendency, not a universal rule: the result depends on the model, the calibration instruments and the structure of the Bermudan. Without recalibration the effect reverses: a higher \lambda with frozen \sigma just damps the volatility, and every price falls.

In one sentence: "After recalibration to the Europeans, higher mean reversion tends to increase the value of the Bermudan, because it reduces the dependence between exercise opportunities and makes the choice of the date more valuable."

1. The HJM framework and the separable volatility

Heath, Jarrow and Morton model the whole curve of instantaneous forward rates f(t,T). Under the risk-neutral measure \mathbb Q, absence of arbitrage (discounted bonds are martingales) fixes the drift once the volatility is chosen:

df(t,T)=\sigma_f(t,T)\left(\int_t^T\sigma_f(t,u)\,du\right)dt+\sigma_f(t,T)\,dW_t .

The model uses the separable volatility \sigma_f(t,T)=\sigma(t)\,e^{-\lambda(T-t)}: a shock at time t moves the forwards of all maturities, exponentially less for distant ones. Writing \sigma_f(t,T)=\eta(t)\,b(T) with \eta(t)=\sigma(t)e^{\lambda t} and b(T)=e^{-\lambda T} makes the model Markovian in a single, driftless Gaussian factor:

X_t=\int_0^t\eta(u)\,dW_u,\qquad X_t\sim\mathcal N\big(0,\alpha(t)\big),\qquad \alpha(t)=\int_0^t\sigma(u)^2e^{2\lambda u}\,du .

This is exactly the Hull–White model dr_t=(\theta(t)-\lambda r_t)\,dt+\sigma(t)\,dW_t with the curve fitted by construction: \lambda is the speed of mean reversion of the short rate, \sigma(t) its instantaneous normal volatility.

2. Zero-coupon bonds and the short rate

Integrating the drift with \bar B(t)=\int_0^tb(u)\,du=(1-e^{-\lambda t})/\lambda and \gamma(t)=\int_0^t\eta(u)^2\bar B(u)\,du gives

f(t,T)=f(0,T)+b(T)\big[\bar B(T)\,\alpha(t)-\gamma(t)+X_t\big],

hence the short rate r_t=f(t,t) and, using \int_t^Tb(u)\bar B(u)\,du=\tfrac12\big(\bar B(T)^2-\bar B(t)^2\big), the bond price P(t,T)=\exp\!\big(-\int_t^Tf(t,u)\,du\big):

\begin{aligned} r_t&=f(0,t)+e^{-\lambda t}\big[\bar B(t)\,\alpha(t)-\gamma(t)\big]+e^{-\lambda t}X_t,\\ P(t,T)&=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\!\Big(-D(t,T)\big[c(t,T)+X_t\big]\Big),\quad D(t,T)=\bar B(T)-\bar B(t),\\ c(t,T)&=\tfrac12\big(\bar B(T)+\bar B(t)\big)\alpha(t)-\gamma(t). \end{aligned}

Bonds are exponential-affine in X_t and decreasing in it: a high factor means high rates. The term c(t,T) is the convexity adjustment that makes P(t,T)/\beta_t, with \beta_t=e^{\int_0^tr_s\,ds}, a martingale. Its sign is exactly what the original code got wrong (section 8).

3. European swaptions: Bachelier and Jamshidian

A payer swaption with expiry T_0, fixed rate K and fixed dates T_1,\dots,T_n (accruals \tau_i) pays \big(1-P(T_0,T_n)-K\sum_i\tau_iP(T_0,T_i)\big)^+. The market quotes it in normal (Bachelier) volatility \sigma_N, with annuity A_0=\sum_i\tau_iP(0,T_i) and forward swap rate S_0=(P(0,T_0)-P(0,T_n))/A_0:

V^{\text{payer}}_0=A_0\Big[(S_0-K)\,\Phi(d)+\sigma_N\sqrt{T_0}\,\varphi(d)\Big],\qquad d=\frac{S_0-K}{\sigma_N\sqrt{T_0}} .

In the model, the payoff is a put with strike 1 on a coupon bond \sum_ic_iP(T_0,T_i), c_i=K\tau_i, c_n=1+K\tau_n. Since every P(T_0,T_i) decreases in the single factor, Jamshidian's trick applies: find x^\star with \sum_ic_iP(T_0,T_i;x^\star)=1, set K_i=P(T_0,T_i;x^\star), and

V^{\text{payer}}_0=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\,\mathrm{ZBP}(T_0,T_i,K_i),\qquad \mathrm{ZBP}=K_iP(0,T_0)\Phi(-h+v)-P(0,T_i)\Phi(-h),

with v=D(T_0,T_i)\sqrt{\alpha(T_0)} and h=\ln\frac{P(0,T_i)}{K_iP(0,T_0)}/v+v/2. The receiver uses calls on the bonds; payer − receiver = forward swap is checked to 10^{-8}.

4. Calibration: the co-terminal bootstrap

Each exercise date T_k carries the European swaption T_k\to T_N. Its model price depends only on \alpha(T_k)=\sum_{j\le k}\sigma_j^2\,\frac{e^{2\lambda T_j}-e^{2\lambda T_{j-1}}}{2\lambda}, i.e. on the segments \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k. Solving the dates in order, each step is a one-dimensional root search (Brent) for \sigma_k, and the fit is exact. The co-terminal Europeans cannot identify \lambda: for any \lambda there is a \sigma(\cdot) that matches them. \lambda is chosen outside the calibration (historical correlations, or other instruments such as caps or diagonal swaptions), which is why its effect is studied in its own tab.

5. The Bermudan as an optimal stopping problem

The holder may enter the swap at any T_k, k=1,\dots,m; the exercise value is the underlying co-terminal swap h_k(x)=\big(1-P(T_k,T_N)-K\sum_{T_i>T_k}\tau_iP(T_k,T_i)\big)^+. By dynamic programming,

V_{T_m}=h_m,\qquad V_{T_k}=\max\big(h_k,\;C_k\big),\qquad C_k=\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[e^{-\int_{T_k}^{T_{k+1}}r_s\,ds}\,V_{T_{k+1}}\,\middle|\,X_{T_k}\right],

and V_0\ge\max_kV^{\text{Eur}}_k: the difference is the early-exercise (switch) premium.

6. The PDE

Between exercise dates, by Feynman–Kac, V(t,x) solves

\partial_tV+\tfrac12\eta(t)^2\,\partial_{xx}V-r(t,x)\,V=0,\qquad r(t,x)=A(t)+e^{-\lambda t}x,

solved backwards on x\in[-n\sqrt{\alpha(T_m)},\,n\sqrt{\alpha(T_m)}] with Dirichlet values equal to the intrinsic value, and V\leftarrow\max(V,h_k) at each T_k (the exercise dates lie on the time grid). Each step solves a tridiagonal system (Thomas algorithm, O(n_x)). The \theta-scheme

\frac{V^n-V^{n+1}}{\Delta t}=\theta\,\mathcal L^nV^n+(1-\theta)\,\mathcal L^{n+1}V^{n+1}

is first order for \theta=1 (implicit Euler, the original code) and second order for \theta=\tfrac12 (Crank–Nicolson). Crank–Nicolson is not L-stable: the kink of the payoff at each exercise date excites oscillations, so a few implicit Rannacher steps are taken after every date to damp them while keeping second order.

7. Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo

Paths of X are simulated exactly (Gaussian increments of variance \alpha(T_{k+1})-\alpha(T_k)). Going backwards, the continuation value is estimated by least squares among in-the-money paths, on a polynomial basis \{1,\tilde x,\dots,\tilde x^d\} with \tilde x=x/\sqrt{\alpha(T_k)} (better conditioned); the path exercises when h_k>\hat C_k. Two biases pull in opposite directions:

  • in-sample (high) bias: regressing and pricing on the same paths lets the rule peek at the future;
  • sub-optimality (low) bias: any exercise rule applied to independent paths gives a lower bound of the true price.

The page offers both (option "Regress on independent paths"). Discounting must use the simulated \int r_s\,ds: the page integrates r by the trapezoidal rule on a fine grid, path by path.

8. Code review: the sign error and other findings

Sign error

hjm_1f.py writes P(t,T)=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}\exp\!\big(D(t,T)[c(t,T)-X_t]\big): the convexity term c(t,T) has the wrong sign, and short_rate, which is its derivative in T at T=t, inherits it. Section 2 derives the correct sign directly from the HJM drift.

  • Receiver swaption. The Python returns the payer formula; here Jamshidian with calls on the bonds.
  • Freedom. User-set \sigma(t) without calibration, implied normal vols of the model, every grid and numerical choice adjustable.

9. Validation

With the notebook settings, the JavaScript engine reproduces every PDE price of Main.ipynb to the 8th decimal (grids, domains, \lambda from 0 to 0.3, strikes from 2 % to 4 %) and the calibrated \sigma to 10^{-17}. With the corrected formula, Jamshidian, the PDE and the Monte Carlo agree on the European, and the simulated bonds recover the curve within their confidence interval.
