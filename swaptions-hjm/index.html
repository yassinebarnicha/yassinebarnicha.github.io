Co-terminal Bermudan swaption pricer

The HJM model (mean reversion λ, piecewise-constant volatility σ(t)) is calibrated live to co-terminal European swaptions quoted in normal volatility; the Bermudan is then priced by a PDE (one factor) or a 2-D backward induction (two factors) and checked by Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo. The two-factor model gives up Jamshidian's trick but fits the whole swaption matrix: it is tested here on a real EUR market. Remark: mean reversion, recalibration and the value of the Bermudan

For a Bermudan swaption, increasing the mean reversion λ and recalibrating to the European swaptions generally increases its price. Why?

  • Recalibration keeps the European prices fixed: the rise in λ is offset by a rise in σ. Each European only sees the total variance of its own exercise date, so its price does not move.
  • Faster mean reversion makes rates less persistent and lowers their correlation across exercise dates: corr(XTi​​,XTj​​)=α(Ti​)/α(Tj​)​, and for a constant σ, α(Ti​)/α(Tj​)=(e2λTi​−1)/(e2λTj​−1) decreases in λ.
  • Choosing among several dates becomes more valuable: if exercising is not worth it today, conditions may be very different at a later date. The Bermudan is worth at least the most expensive European, and the gap (the switch option) grows when the dates decorrelate.

The Bermudan therefore gains more from its exercise flexibility, while the calibration Europeans keep their prices. It is a tendency, not a universal rule: the result depends on the model, the calibration instruments and the structure of the Bermudan. Without recalibration the effect reverses: a higher λ with frozen σ just damps the volatility, and every price falls.

In one sentence: "After recalibration to the Europeans, higher mean reversion tends to increase the value of the Bermudan, because it reduces the dependence between exercise opportunities and makes the choice of the date more valuable."

1. The HJM framework and the separable volatility

Heath, Jarrow and Morton model the whole curve of instantaneous forward rates f(t,T). Under the risk-neutral measure Q, absence of arbitrage (discounted bonds are martingales) fixes the drift once the volatility is chosen:

df(t,T)=σf​(t,T)(∫tT​σf​(t,u)du)dt+σf​(t,T)dWt​.

The model uses the separable volatility σf​(t,T)=σ(t)e−λ(T−t): a shock at time t moves the forwards of all maturities, exponentially less for distant ones. Writing σf​(t,T)=η(t)b(T) with η(t)=σ(t)eλt and b(T)=e−λT makes the model Markovian in a single, driftless Gaussian factor:

Xt​=∫0t​η(u)dWu​,Xt​∼N(0,α(t)),α(t)=∫0t​σ(u)2e2λudu.

This is exactly the Hull–White model drt​=(θ(t)−λrt​)dt+σ(t)dWt​ with the curve fitted by construction: λ is the speed of mean reversion of the short rate, σ(t) its instantaneous normal volatility.

2. Zero-coupon bonds and the short rate

Integrating the drift with Bˉ(t)=∫0t​b(u)du=(1−e−λt)/λ and γ(t)=∫0t​η(u)2Bˉ(u)du gives

f(t,T)=f(0,T)+b(T)[Bˉ(T)α(t)−γ(t)+Xt​],

hence the short rate rt​=f(t,t) and, using ∫tT​b(u)Bˉ(u)du=21​(Bˉ(T)2−Bˉ(t)2), the bond price P(t,T)=exp(−∫tT​f(t,u)du):

rt​P(t,T)c(t,T)​=f(0,t)+e−λt[Bˉ(t)α(t)−γ(t)]+e−λtXt​,=P(0,t)P(0,T)​exp(−D(t,T)[c(t,T)+Xt​]),D(t,T)=Bˉ(T)−Bˉ(t),=21​(Bˉ(T)+Bˉ(t))α(t)−γ(t).​

Bonds are exponential-affine in Xt​ and decreasing in it: a high factor means high rates. The term c(t,T) is the convexity adjustment that makes P(t,T)/βt​, with βt​=e∫0t​rs​ds, a martingale. Its sign is exactly what the original code got wrong (section 8).

3. European swaptions: Bachelier and Jamshidian

A payer swaption with expiry T0​, fixed rate K and fixed dates T1​,…,Tn​ (accruals τi​) pays (1−P(T0​,Tn​)−K∑i​τi​P(T0​,Ti​))+. The market quotes it in normal (Bachelier) volatility σN​, with annuity A0​=∑i​τi​P(0,Ti​) and forward swap rate S0​=(P(0,T0​)−P(0,Tn​))/A0​:

V0payer​=A0​[(S0​−K)Φ(d)+σN​T0​​φ(d)],d=σN​T0​​S0​−K​.

In the model, the payoff is a put with strike 1 on a coupon bond ∑i​ci​P(T0​,Ti​), ci​=Kτi​, cn​=1+Kτn​. Since every P(T0​,Ti​) decreases in the single factor, Jamshidian's trick applies: find x⋆ with ∑i​ci​P(T0​,Ti​;x⋆)=1, set Ki​=P(T0​,Ti​;x⋆), and

V0payer​=i=1∑n​ci​ZBP(T0​,Ti​,Ki​),ZBP=Ki​P(0,T0​)Φ(−h+v)−P(0,Ti​)Φ(−h),

with v=D(T0​,Ti​)α(T0​)​ and h=lnKi​P(0,T0​)P(0,Ti​)​/v+v/2. The receiver uses calls on the bonds; payer − receiver = forward swap is checked to 10−8.

4. Calibration: the co-terminal bootstrap

Each exercise date Tk​ carries the European swaption Tk​→TN​. Its model price depends only on α(Tk​)=∑j≤k​σj2​2λe2λTj​−e2λTj−1​​, i.e. on the segments σ1​,…,σk​. Solving the dates in order, each step is a one-dimensional root search (Brent) for σk​, and the fit is exact. The co-terminal Europeans cannot identify λ: for any λ there is a σ(⋅) that matches them. λ is chosen outside the calibration (historical correlations, or other instruments such as caps or diagonal swaptions), which is why its effect is studied in its own tab.

5. The Bermudan as an optimal stopping problem

The holder may enter the swap at any Tk​, k=1,…,m; the exercise value is the underlying co-terminal swap hk​(x)=(1−P(Tk​,TN​)−K∑Ti​>Tk​​τi​P(Tk​,Ti​))+. By dynamic programming,

VTm​​=hm​,VTk​​=max(hk​,Ck​),Ck​=EQ[e−∫Tk​Tk+1​​rs​dsVTk+1​​​XTk​​],

and V0​≥maxk​VkEur​: the difference is the early-exercise (switch) premium.

6. The PDE

Between exercise dates, by Feynman–Kac, V(t,x) solves

∂t​V+21​η(t)2∂xx​V−r(t,x)V=0,r(t,x)=A(t)+e−λtx,

solved backwards on x∈[−nα(Tm​)​,nα(Tm​)​] with Dirichlet values equal to the intrinsic value, and V←max(V,hk​) at each Tk​ (the exercise dates lie on the time grid). Each step solves a tridiagonal system (Thomas algorithm, O(nx​)). The θ-scheme

ΔtVn−Vn+1​=θLnVn+(1−θ)Ln+1Vn+1

is first order for θ=1 (implicit Euler, the original code) and second order for θ=21​ (Crank–Nicolson). Crank–Nicolson is not L-stable: the kink of the payoff at each exercise date excites oscillations, so a few implicit Rannacher steps are taken after every date to damp them while keeping second order.

7. Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo

Paths of X are simulated exactly (Gaussian increments of variance α(Tk+1​)−α(Tk​)). Going backwards, the continuation value is estimated by least squares among in-the-money paths, on a polynomial basis {1,x~,…,x~d} with x~=x/α(Tk​)​ (better conditioned); the path exercises when hk​>C^k​. Two biases pull in opposite directions:

  • in-sample (high) bias: regressing and pricing on the same paths lets the rule peek at the future;
  • sub-optimality (low) bias: any exercise rule applied to independent paths gives a lower bound of the true price.

The page offers both (option "Regress on independent paths"). Discounting must use the simulated ∫rs​ds: the page integrates r by the trapezoidal rule on a fine grid, path by path.

Remark: the Europeans do not determine the Bermudan

With one factor, once the co-terminal Europeans are matched, the Bermudan is almost fixed: only λ is left, and its effect is studied in the one-factor Mean reversion tab. With two factors, the same Europeans are compatible with very different Bermudans: what matters is how the swap rate at one exercise date co-moves with the swap rate at the next, and that is set by ρ, λ2​ and σ2​/σ1​, which the co-terminals do not see.

  • The premium moves with the dependence structure: with the default inputs the exercise premium (Bermudan − most expensive European) goes from about 19 bp at ρ=−0.95 to about 29 bp at ρ=+0.9, against 24 bp for one factor. The mechanism is the one of the one-factor Mean reversion tab: how much the swap rates of successive exercise dates move together decides how much choosing the date is worth.
  • This is model risk made visible, not a defect: it is exactly the freedom that lets the model fit swaptions of other tenors, or a quoted Bermudan price.

1. From one factor to two

The forward rates are driven by two Brownian motions with correlation ρ, and each factor keeps the separable exponential volatility of the one-factor model:

σk​(t,T)=σk​(t)e−λk​(T−t),k=1,2,d⟨W1,W2⟩t​=ρdt.

The no-arbitrage drift is ∑k,l​ρkl​σk​(t,T)∫tT​σl​(t,u)du (with ρkk​=1, ρ12​=ρ). Exactly as before, the model is Markovian in two driftless Gaussian factors Xtk​=∫0t​σk​(u)eλk​udWuk​, jointly normal with

Cov(Xtk​,Xtl​)=Akl​(t)=ρkl​∫0t​σk​(u)σl​(u)e(λk​+λl​)udu.

This is the two-factor Hull–White model of the lecture notes (r=r0​+r1​+r2​, drk​=−λk​rk​dt+σk​dWk), where a negative ρ (around −0.9) lets the first factor carry the level of the curve and the second its slope. With σ2​=0 it is the one-factor model of the other tab, exactly.

2. Zero-coupon bonds

With Bˉk​(t)=(1−e−λk​t)/λk​, Dk​(t,T)=Bˉk​(T)−Bˉk​(t) and Gkl​(t)=ρkl​∫0t​σk​σl​e(λk​+λl​)uBˉl​(u)du,

P(t,T)=P(0,t)P(0,T)​exp(−k∑​Dk​(t,T)Xtk​+κ(t,T)),
κ(t,T)=−21​k,l∑​[(Bˉk​(T)Bˉl​(T)−Bˉk​(t)Bˉl​(t))Akl​(t)−Dk​(t,T)Gkl​(t)−Dl​(t,T)Glk​(t)].

For one factor κ=−Dc: this is the corrected formula of the other tab (convexity term with the sign of Xt​). The check that matters is the martingale defect ET[P(t,b)/P(t,T)]−P(0,b)/P(0,T), computed with the closed-form drift of the factors under the T-forward measure: it is zero to machine precision for any parameters.

3. What replaces Jamshidian

Jamshidian's trick rests on one fact: the swap value 1−P(T0​,Tn​)−K∑i​τi​P(T0​,Ti​) is monotone in the single factor, so a single root x⋆ splits it into options on zero-coupon bonds. With two factors the swap value is a function S(x1​,x2​) of the plane and the exercise set {S>0} is bounded by a curve: there is no threshold, no common strike Ki​, no decomposition (the region plot of the Pricer tab shows the curve). The price is instead the integral

V0​=P(0,T0​)ET0​[S(XT0​​)+],XT0​​∼N(m(T0​;T0​),A(T0​)),

where the mean mk​(t;T)=−∑l​ρkl​∫0t​σk​σl​eλk​uBˉl​(T−u)du is the (deterministic) drift of the factors under the T-forward measure. The page evaluates it on a fine uniform grid of the normal law (default 161 × 161 points): the payoff has a kink, and on a kink a fine uniform rule is more accurate than Gauss–Hermite (measured: 4⋅10−4 relative against Jamshidian in the one-factor limit). The bootstrap still works because the price increases with the volatility level, but every Brent step now costs an integral instead of a closed form.

Closed-form approximations exist (conditioning on one factor then applying a Jamshidian-type argument on the other, or Gaussian approximations of the swap rate): they are faster but approximate, and are not used here.

4. Calibration: what the Europeans can and cannot identify

The co-terminal bootstrap is unchanged: for given (λ1​,λ2​,ρ,σ2​/σ1​), one root per exercise date fixes the level of σ1​, and the fit is exact. But these structural parameters are invisible to the co-terminals: any value of them can be compensated by the level. Identifying them needs swaptions of other tenors, and that is a global, non-linear fit (Nelder–Mead on the root-mean-square gap, the level being re-bootstrapped at every evaluation).

The fitted ρ is close to −1 and the parameters sit in a flat valley: three of five starting points reach the same fit (2.58 to 2.62 bp) with λ2​ between 0.92 and 1.03, ρ between −0.98 and −1.00 and a ratio between 2.65 and 3.0, while two others stall against the bounds (7.2 bp, with λ2​=3, ρ=−1; and 11.1 bp, which is the one-factor solution). 5. The Bermudan on a two-dimensional grid

The one-factor PDE is a tridiagonal system per time step. In two factors the PDE has a cross-derivative and costs a grid of n2 points per step. But between exercise dates the factors are exactly Gaussian, so the page does not discretise time at all. Working under the TN​-forward measure, on values deflated by P(t,TN​) (martingales: no discount factor to simulate),

Uj​(x)=max(P(Tj​,TN​;x)hj​(x)​,ETN​[Uj+1​(XTj+1​​)​XTj​​=x]),V0​=P(0,TN​)ETN​[U1​(XT1​​)],

where XTj+1​​−XTj​​∼N(m(Tj+1​;TN​)−m(Tj​;TN​),A(Tj+1​)−A(Tj​)) independently of the past. The conditional expectation is a Gaussian kernel integrated against bilinear interpolation of Uj+1​ on its grid. The only errors are the grid and the kernel quadrature (measured: 1.2 % at 41 × 61, 0.2 % at 101 × 141). The Longstaff-Schwartz engine runs on the same deflated values with a two-variable polynomial basis, and brackets the quadrature from below (suboptimal exercise rule).

7. Validation

  • No arbitrage: the martingale defect is zero (10−17) for generic parameters; the bond formula and the drift of the factors agree.
  • One-factor limit: with σ2​=0, the European 2-D integral reproduces Jamshidian (4⋅10−4 relative) and the quadrature reproduces the corrected one-factor PDE (0.2 %).
  • Perfect correlation: ρ=1 with λ1​=λ2​ and σ1​+σ2​=σ gives the one-factor price.
  • Two engines: quadrature and Monte Carlo agree within 0.4 % on four scenarios (the quadrature usually slightly above, the Monte Carlo below: its exercise rule is suboptimal).
  • Two languages: the JavaScript engine of this page and the Python package hjm2f agree to 10−16 on prices, covariances and bootstrap, and find the same global fit on the real data.
