Remark: why the Δ of a floating-strike lookback is constant at issue

Under Black-Scholes the whole path is proportional to the spot: S_t=S\,e^{(r-q-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma W_t}. At issue the starting extremum is the spot itself, so S factors out of the whole payoff:

S_T-\min_{[0,T]}S=S\Big(\tfrac{S_T}{S}-\min_{[0,T]}\tfrac{S_t}{S}\Big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(S)=S\,f(r,q,\sigma,T),

where f does not depend on S. The issue price is linear in S (homogeneous of degree 1), hence:

  • \Delta=\partial V/\partial S=f=V/S: the same for every spot; it is read directly from the price, with no finite difference;
  • Vega, ρ and Θ are proportional to S: doubling the spot doubles every other Greek;
  • the second derivative of the issue price S\mapsto V(S) is zero, which is what the engines report: they bump the spot and the starting extremum together, and on common random numbers each path's payoff is itself linear in S, so their Γ is exactly zero up to rounding.

A subtlety for hedging. Once the option is held, the extremum m is fixed and the price is V(S,m)=S\,g(m/S), homogeneous in (S,m) jointly. By Euler, V=S\,\partial_SV+m\,\partial_mV, so \Delta=V/S-(m/S)\,\partial_mV. At m=S, \partial_mV=0 (the path immediately sets a new extremum, so the old one no longer matters): the hedging Δ is still V/S. But the hedging Γ is \partial_{SS}V=g''(1)/S, not zero: 0.0312 for the put and 0.0455 for the call at S=100, r=5\,\%, \sigma=20\,\%, T=1. The chart "Γ" shows both.

In one sentence: "At issue the price of a floating-strike lookback is homogeneous of degree 1 in S: S comes out of the expectation, so Δ = V/S is constant; the issue price has no curvature, but a contract already held, whose extremum stays fixed, does — its Γ is g''(1)/S > 0."

1. The contract

A European floating-strike lookback, under Black-Scholes with continuous dividend q (carry b=r-q):

\text{call: }S_T-\min\Big(m,\min_{t\le s\le T}S_s\Big),\qquad \text{put: }\max\Big(M,\max_{t\le s\le T}S_s\Big)-S_T,

where m (or M) is the extremum already reached; at issue, m=M=S. The holder buys at the lowest price (call) or sells at the highest (put) over the life of the option: the payoff is always non-negative.

2. The law of the running extremum

Write X_t=\ln(S_t/S)=\mu t+\sigma W_t, \mu=b-\sigma^2/2, and M_T=\max_{[0,T]}X. The reflection principle, with a Girsanov change of measure to remove the drift, gives

\mathbb P(M_T\le y)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{y-\mu T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right)-e^{2\mu y/\sigma^2}\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{-y-\mu T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right),\qquad y\ge0,

and, conditionally on the end point, the Brownian bridge law, which does not depend on the drift:

\mathbb P\big(M_\tau\ge y\,\big|\,X_0=x_0,\,X_\tau=x_1\big)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{2(y-x_0)(y-x_1)}{\sigma^2\tau}\right),\qquad y\ge\max(x_0,x_1).

The page plots the first one against the simulated extrema; the second one is the heart of both Monte Carlo engines.

3. Closed form (Goldman, Sosin and Gatto)

Since V^{\text{put}}=e^{-rT}\,\mathbb E\big[\max(M,S\,e^{M_T})\big]-S\,e^{-qT}, integrating against the law above gives, with b_1=\frac{\ln(S/M)+(b+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T} and b_2=b_1-\sigma\sqrt T,

V^{\text{put}}=-S\,e^{(b-r)T}\Phi(-b_1)+M e^{-rT}\Phi(-b_2)+S\,e^{-rT}\frac{\sigma^2}{2b}\Big[-\Big(\frac SM\Big)^{-2b/\sigma^2}\Phi\Big(b_1-\frac{2b}{\sigma}\sqrt T\Big)+e^{bT}\Phi(b_1)\Big],

and symmetrically for the call. The limit b\to0 is finite (the page averages b=\pm10^{-6}; the Barnicha code has the exact limit). At issue and without dividend, each engine uses its own code's formula; the general form agrees to 10^{-10}.

4. Homogeneity and the Greeks

Because the dynamics is multiplicative, V(\lambda S,\lambda m)=\lambda V(S,m). Hence V=S\,\partial_SV+m\,\partial_mV (Euler) and, since \partial_mV=0 at m=S, \Delta=V/S at issue, whether one differentiates the issue price or the contract held. Writing V(S,m)=S\,g(m/S),

\Delta=g(z)-z\,g'(z),\qquad\Gamma=\frac{z^2g''(z)}{S},\qquad z=\frac mS,

so at issue \Delta=g(1) and the hedging Γ is g''(1)/S>0, while the issue price S\mapsto S\,g(1) is a straight line. Vega, ρ and Θ are proportional to S. See the "Constant Δ" tab.

5. Engine A: exact Brownian bridge in one step (Barnicha)

Draw X_T=\mu T+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z exactly, then the extremum of the bridge by inverting the law above with a uniform U:

M_T=\tfrac12\Big(X_T+\sqrt{X_T^2-2\sigma^2T\ln U}\Big),\qquad m_T=\tfrac12\Big(X_T-\sqrt{X_T^2-2\sigma^2T\ln U'}\Big).

One step, no discretisation bias, and the antithetic pair (Z,-Z) divides the variance by about 2 (standard error ÷ 1.42 on the default case).

6. Engine B: time stepping, BGK and the bridge per step (Chivet)

Paths on n steps of length \Delta t=T/n, extremum read at the grid dates: it misses the excursions between dates, a bias of order \sqrt{\Delta t} (-1.25 on a price of 14.29 with 100 steps). Three choices:

  • no correction: discrete monitoring, as is;
  • BGK (the C++, Broadie, Glasserman and Kou): \max S\leftarrow e^{\beta\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\max S with \beta=-\zeta(\tfrac12)/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx0.5826, an asymptotic correction whose residual bias is of order \Delta t;
  • exact bridge per step (added): on each step, the bridge extremum is drawn from the law of section 2. The scheme is then exact for continuous monitoring, whatever the number of steps.

7. Monte Carlo Greeks

Greeks are computed by bump and revalue with common random numbers: the bumped prices reuse the same draws, so for a payoff Lipschitz in the parameter the variance of (V(\theta+h)-V(\theta-h))/2h stays bounded as h\to0 instead of growing like 1/h^2. At issue each path's payoff is linear in S, so the bumped Δ equals V/S path by path and the bumped Γ is zero up to rounding.

8. Code review: defects found

  • The Barnicha program does not compile: Barnicha/app/main.cpp has using namespace lookback; while the namespace is called Barnicha. It is the executable the Excel macros call.
  • Chivet ignores the seed: getRandomSeed() returns the clock, so two identical runs differ. Here the seed entered is used.
  • Chivet: imperfect common random numbers. setRandomSeed reseeds the generator, but the normal law keeps the value held in reserve by the polar method: bumped prices do not start from the same numbers, which adds noise to the Greeks. Here the law restarts at each price.
  • Chivet: wrong standard error of ρ. The estimate divides by 2\,h\,r, the standard error only by 2h. Fixed here.
  • Chivet: closed-form Γ four times too small. Divided by (0.0002\,S)^2 instead of (0.0001\,S)^2; invisible at issue, where the issue-price Γ is 0. Fixed here.
  • Chivet: r = 0 not allowed. The relative bump on r and the closed form (division by 2r) are undefined: the page shows "undefined". The Barnicha engine handles the limit r\to0.
  • Opposite Θ conventions: Barnicha computes (V(T-h)-V(T))/h, one-sided; Chivet +\partial V/\partial T, centred. Option here: centred Θ for Barnicha.
  • Γ at issue is not the hedging Γ: both engines bump the spot and the starting extremum together, which gives the curvature of the issue price (zero), not that of a contract held (g''(1)/S). The seasoned mode gives the latter.
  • Barnicha draws u_{\max} and u_{\min} independently: correct for these contracts, which use only one of the two extrema. A payoff using both (max − min) would need their joint law.
  • out_size is never set by the two exported functions: the caller must know the size (48).

9. Validation of the port

The generator std::mt19937 and libstdc++'s distributions are reproduced bit for bit. With the same seed, the Barnicha engine returns the prices of the C++ program compiled with g++ to 10^{-15}, and the Chivet engine those of the C++ simulator (with and without BGK) to 10^{-12}. The closed-form Greeks are those of the Excel workbook to 10^{-10}. With a dividend or a seasoned extremum, the Monte Carlo recovers the general formula within its confidence interval, and the bridge per step shows no visible bias even with 2 steps. The full report gives the proofs and the numerical study.
